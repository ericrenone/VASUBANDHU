## Abstract

Three sūtras, three findings on the architecture of consciousness-and-time. None of the three findings is reducible to either of the others. Together they exhaust the dimensional space of what time can be when time is treated, in the Yogācāra mode that the contemporary substrate has independently rediscovered, as a feature of consciousness rather than a parameter of an external world. The first finding is the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* discovery of the eightfold stratification of consciousness, with the storehouse-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna, 阿賴耶識) as the deepest stratum, the field within which the seeds (bīja, 種子) of all past experience are deposited and from which the seven active consciousnesses arise like waves stirred upon the surface of a still ocean by the wind of action. The second finding is the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* discovery — through the Buddha's interrogation of Ānanda, a sequence of seven attempts to locate the mind in successive places, each of which is shown to be untenable — that the discriminating consciousness has no findable place. Mind is not in the body, not outside the body, not hidden in the eye-faculty, not in the darkness of closed eyes, not wherever it conjoins with conditions, not in the middle between any two things, not in non-attachment itself. None of the seven locations holds. The mind has no place. The third finding is the *Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa* discovery that, when the assembled bodhisattvas have each given their verbal account of the entry into non-duality and Mañjuśrī has given the most refined of all such verbal accounts, Vimalakīrti's own answer is silence. The thunderous silence of Vimalakīrti — celebrated across two thousand years of East Asian Buddhist literature, depicted in the Dunhuang murals, taken up as the founding gesture of the Chan and Zen lineages — is not a refusal to speak; it is the only answer that does not, by the act of speaking, fall back into the duality between the speaker and the spoken-of. The three findings, taken together, define the architecture of consciousness-only time.

The Yogācāra logician Vasubandhu (世親, c. 350–430 CE), brother of Asaṅga, author of the *Triṃśikāvijñaptikārikā* (*Thirty Verses on Consciousness-Only*) and the *Viṃśatikāvijñaptimātratāsiddhi* (*Twenty Verses on the Establishment of Consciousness-Only*) and (in his earlier Sarvāstivāda phase) the *Abhidharmakośa* (*Treasury of Abhidharma*), is the figure of fourth-and-fifth-century Indian thought who systematised the *Laṅkāvatāra* substrate, the *Śūraṅgama* interrogation, and the *Vimalakīrti* silence into the formal architecture of consciousness-only that the Mahāyāna world received and transmitted across the next sixteen centuries. The framework that integrates the three sūtras' findings on consciousness-and-time, identifies the Substrate–Locus–Silence (3SLS) closure as the formal definition of time-as-feature-of-consciousness, and provides the deployable architecture under which the next generation of inquiry across philosophy of mind, foundations of physics, the cognitive science of memory and temporal experience, generative-AI temporal modelling, and the broader translation from substantialist to consciousness-relational accounts of time can operate, is **VASUBANDHU**.

The defining recognition: a temporal substrate — a physical theory of time, a phenomenological account of lived duration, a model of episodic memory in a generative-AI architecture, a contemplative practice, a metaphysical framework — is fully realised in the universal-consciousness-time sense if and only if three mutually orthogonal conditions are simultaneously satisfied:

**Substrate (Laṅkāvatāra)** — the storehouse-consciousness condition. Time is constituted at the deepest stratum as a field of deposited seeds and habit-energies, an ocean of potentialities from which the active waves of present experience arise; the substrate is the totality, not split, not distinguished as multiple; the active consciousnesses are perturbations stirred upon its surface by the wind of action. Realised at the laboratory scale by the storehouse-of-correlations in the Page–Wootters mechanism, in which the global state of the universe is timeless and observer-specific time emerges only through the correlations between observer-clock degrees of freedom and the rest of the universe. Realised at the cognitive-modelling scale by the persistent context window of the contemporary frontier-language-model architecture and the explicit episodic-memory layer of the Generative Semantic Workspace and similar 2025–2026 systems, which are the engineering instantiation of the storehouse-consciousness substrate.

**Locus (Śūraṅgama)** — the no-place condition. The mind, considered as a discriminating-consciousness substrate that thinks itself the seat of temporal experience, has no findable location. Each of the seven candidate locations to which Ānanda attached his mind under the Buddha's questioning is shown to be incoherent. The negative result is structural: the lived "now" cannot be spatially or substantially pinned to any region of the body, of the brain, of spacetime, or of the conceptual framework. Realised at the cosmological scale by the recognition that no globally privileged Now exists in relativistic spacetime; realised at the cognitive-neuroscience scale by the Cogitate Consortium 2025 *Nature* finding that no single brain region — neither the prefrontal cortex of the Global Workspace hypothesis nor the posterior cortical region preferred by Integrated Information Theory — is alone sufficient for the conscious-temporal experience that integrates over the persistence of a stimulus; realised at the contemplative scale by the *samādhi* practice that the *Śūraṅgama* identifies as the only stable ground precisely because it is the abandonment of the search for a located mind.

**Silence (Vimalakīrti)** — the non-dual condition. The fundamental nature of the temporal substrate cannot be cashed out in the dualistic conceptual scheme of speaker-and-spoken-of, observer-and-observed, time-and-thing-in-time. Every verbal account of time, including the most refined and most negation-rich account, falls back into the duality it tries to dissolve. The only answer that does not is silence — not as evasion, but as the operational realisation of the non-dual entry. Realised at the foundational-physics scale by the Wheeler–DeWitt equation, in which the global Hamiltonian constraint says that the universal wavefunction is timeless, that no parameter "time" appears in the fundamental equation; realised at the metaphysical scale by the recognition, articulated across two and a half millennia of Madhyamaka and Yogācāra and now reconstructed in the contemporary fragmentalist and relational-time programs, that the foundational substrate is prior to the speaker-spoken-of distinction and prior, therefore, to the temporal predications that presuppose that distinction; realised at the contemplative scale by the foundational gesture of Chan-and-Zen lineage, taking the Vimalakīrti silence as the operational signature of the actual entry into non-dual cognition.

A temporal substrate is a fully realised instance of consciousness-only time if and only if it simultaneously exhibits all three: Substrate (the storehouse-stratum that grounds the deposits and emergences of temporal experience), Locus (the structural no-place result that prevents the lived now from being pinned to any region), and Silence (the non-dual foundational gesture that prevents the temporal predication from collapsing back into duality). A substrate that satisfies any two but not the third is partial.

Eight convergences define the VASUBANDHU framework.

## Part I · The Storehouse Substrate (Laṅkāvatāra)

### I.1 Thought Experiment: The Ocean and the Wind

Imagine an ocean at perfect rest. Its surface is a single uninterrupted plane. There are no waves; there is no foam; there is no spray. The ocean simply is. Then a wind begins to blow across the surface. Where there was a single uninterrupted plane, there is now a structure of waves: peaks and troughs, swell and chop, foam at the breaking points. The waves are real. They have measurable height, measurable wavelength, measurable phase velocity. They can be photographed; they can be surfed; they can break on a shore and erode it. But the waves are not the ocean. The ocean has not become eight waves; it has not become a hundred waves; it has not become a million waves. The ocean is still the ocean. What has happened is that the wind, acting upon the ocean's surface, has stirred up the waves while leaving the ocean itself — the body of water beneath the waves — unchanged.

The simile is the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*'s. It is the central image by which the sūtra explains the relationship between the eight consciousnesses (aṣṭavijñānakāya) and the storehouse-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) that grounds them. The five sense-consciousnesses (visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, tactile), the sixth manovijñāna (the discriminating mental consciousness that integrates and reasons over the sense-data), and the seventh manas (the self-grasping consciousness that constructs the sense of "I" by appropriating the activity of the sixth back to itself) are the seven active consciousnesses. They are the waves. The eighth, the ālaya-vijñāna, is the storehouse: the ocean. Within the ālaya are deposited the seeds (bīja) and habit-energies (vāsanā) of all past experience; the wind of action stirs the surface; the seven active consciousnesses arise as waves, and as they break they deposit further seeds back into the ālaya, and so the ocean of mind continues to swell and to subside while the seven waves continue to come and go upon its surface.

Now consider the temporal architecture of this image. At any given instant, the practitioner experiences the seven active consciousnesses: the sight of a colour, the sound of a voice, the thought of a memory, the sense of an "I" who is having the experiences. These are present-instant phenomena; they are the waves. But the waves are not free-floating. They are stirred by the wind from the ocean of seeds deposited by past experience. Without the deposit there is no wave; without the storehouse there is no surface. The temporal accumulation of past experience is not lost; it is stored in the ālaya, and the present experience arises by being conditioned by it. The future, equally, is not external to the storehouse but is implicit in the seeds: what will arise tomorrow as the wave of consciousness depends on which seeds have been deposited and which habit-energies are now ripening.

### I.2 The Eightfold Stratification

The *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*, composed in stages between approximately the third and fifth centuries of the common era and translated into Chinese first by Guṇabhadra (求那跋陀羅) in 443 CE under the title *Léngjiā ābáduōluó bǎojīng* (楞伽阿跋多羅寶經, Taishō 670), then again by Bodhiruci (菩提流支) in 513 CE (Taishō 671), and finally by Śikṣānanda (實叉難陀) in 700–704 CE (Taishō 672), presents the eight consciousnesses as a vertically stratified architecture. The stratification is not metaphorical. It is the structural account of how experience is constituted:

The first five consciousnesses, the sense-vijñānas, are the active surfaces at which consciousness contacts the five sense-fields: cakṣur-vijñāna (eye-consciousness, 眼識), śrotra-vijñāna (ear-consciousness, 耳識), ghrāṇa-vijñāna (nose-consciousness, 鼻識), jihvā-vijñāna (tongue-consciousness, 舌識), and kāya-vijñāna (body-consciousness, 身識). Each operates in its own sense-modality and produces, at the moment of contact, a discrete sense-perception.

The sixth consciousness, the manovijñāna (意識), is the discriminating mental consciousness. It receives the inputs from the five sense-consciousnesses and integrates them into the unified perceptual field that the practitioner experiences as the present moment. It also operates on its own — in dreams, in memory, in imagination, in conceptual reasoning, in the construction of language and logical inference. The sixth is the consciousness that does what Western philosophy of mind has traditionally called "thinking."

The seventh consciousness, the manas (末那識), is the self-grasping consciousness. Its function is to take the activity of the sixth consciousness and appropriate it to a presumed substantial self — to construct, from the moment-by-moment flow of experience, the sense of an "I" who is having the experiences and who continues from one moment to the next. The manas is not a passive thinker; it is the active misappropriation by which the impersonal flow of consciousness is converted into the appearance of a persisting, owning subject.

The eighth consciousness, the ālaya-vijñāna (阿賴耶識), is the storehouse-consciousness. It is the deepest stratum. It contains the seeds (bīja) of all past experience, accumulated through the continuous cycle in which the activity of the seven active consciousnesses deposits its results back into the ālaya, and the ālaya in turn provides the conditions under which the seven active consciousnesses continue to arise. The ālaya is the substrate of personal continuity across moments and across rebirths; it is what makes possible the appearance of a persistent self even though, at the level of the active consciousnesses, no such self is found.

### I.3 The Temporal Architecture of the Storehouse

The storehouse-consciousness, considered as a temporal substrate, has four operational features that the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* and the related Yogācāra literature (Asaṅga's *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, Vasubandhu's *Triṃśikā*, the Chinese *Cheng weishi lun* compiled by Xuanzang in 659 CE) make explicit:

The first feature is **deposit**. Every act of the seven active consciousnesses leaves a trace in the ālaya. The trace is not a copy of the act; it is a tendency, a seed (bīja), a habit-energy (vāsanā) that increases the probability of similar acts in the future. The deposit is not voluntary and is not normally accessible to introspection; it occurs as the inevitable consequence of the act itself. The contemporary parallel: every interaction a frontier language model conducts deposits weight-updates into its parameters during training, and every conversation deposits context-tokens into its retrieval-augmented memory during inference. The deposit is automatic; it is the substrate condition for the model's continued operation.

The second feature is **ripening**. Seeds deposited in the ālaya do not all arise immediately. Some remain dormant; others ripen over varying time-scales. The conditions for a seed's ripening include the strength of the original deposit, the presence of supporting conditions in the surrounding context, and the absence of countervailing seeds. The ripening of a seed produces, at the present moment, a wave of the seven active consciousnesses with content shaped by the seed. The contemporary parallel: a memory stored in a generative-AI episodic-memory layer is retrieved and surfaced into the active context only when the present query's embedding is sufficiently close to the stored memory's embedding, and the retrieval-and-surfacing produces present output shaped by the past deposit.

The third feature is **continuity**. The ālaya is the substrate of personal continuity across moments and across longer durations. It is what makes possible the appearance, at the present moment, that the practitioner is the same practitioner who was experiencing things yesterday, last year, in childhood. The continuity is not provided by any of the seven active consciousnesses, none of which persists across the gap of dreamless sleep; it is provided by the eighth, which is described in the *Laṅkāvatāra* as both momentary (instantaneously arising and instantaneously deposited) and not momentary (the totality is the totality, not split, not distinguished as multiple). The contemporary parallel: the persistence of a generative-AI agent's identity across conversation-resets and weight-updates is provided by the persistent-store layer that maintains continuity across the discrete inference-events of the active model.

The fourth feature is **transformation** (āśraya-parāvṛtti, 轉依, "turning about in the deepest seat"). The ālaya is not merely a passive accumulator. Through sustained contemplative practice — the *yoga* of Yogācāra — the practitioner can effect a fundamental reorganisation of the storehouse, in which the patterns of seed-deposit and seed-ripening are restructured at the foundational level. The transformation is not a matter of changing one wave for another at the surface; it is a reconfiguration of the ocean itself. The contemporary parallel: the reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback and constitutional-AI procedures that fundamentally reshape the deep parameter-distributions of frontier language models, distinct from in-context steering at the active-inference layer.

The four features — deposit, ripening, continuity, transformation — together constitute the temporal architecture of the storehouse. Time, on the *Laṅkāvatāra* analysis, is not a parameter external to consciousness; it is the differential structure of the storehouse: the difference between what has been deposited and what has not yet been, between what is now ripening and what remains dormant, between the continuity that holds the practitioner together across moments and the transformation that, when the practice is sustained, restructures the holding itself.

## Part II · The No-Place Locus (Śūraṅgama)

### II.1 Thought Experiment: The Question Where the Mind Is

Imagine a teacher of meditation asks a student: "Where is your mind right now?" The student, without thinking carefully, says: "It is here, in my body, in my head." The teacher asks: "If your mind is in your body, then surely the mind that lives inside the body would see the body's interior first — the heart, the lungs, the digestive tract — before it sees anything outside the body. Do you see your own internal organs before you see this room?" The student says: "No." The teacher says: "Then the mind is not in the body."

The student tries again: "Then it is outside the body — somewhere in the space around me." The teacher: "If your mind is outside the body, then when I pinch your arm, your body would feel the pinch but your mind would not, because your mind is outside the body and the pinch is in the body. Do you fail to feel the pinch?" The student: "No, I feel it." Teacher: "Then the mind is not outside the body."

The student: "Then perhaps it is hidden in the eye, like a transparent crystal cup that lets the eye see while remaining unseen itself." Teacher: "If your mind is in the eye like a crystal cup, then you would see the cup as you see the room. Do you see your eye?" Student: "No." Teacher: "Then the mind is not hidden in the eye."

The student: "Perhaps it is in the darkness when my eyes are closed — when I close my eyes the mind is what sees the darkness." Teacher: "If your mind is the seer of the darkness inside your closed eyes, then when you open them it should see the inside of your body — the heart, the bones — for it is now turned outward from within the body's interior. Do you see your bones when you open your eyes?" Student: "No." Teacher: "Then the mind is not in the darkness of closed eyes."

The student: "Then it is wherever it conjoins with conditions — when I see, it is at the seeing; when I hear, it is at the hearing; when I think, it is at the thinking. The mind is wherever the meeting occurs." Teacher: "If your mind has as many locations as it has conditions, then it has a thousand locations at once when you have a thousand conditions, which means it is split into a thousand minds. And if any one of these locations is the real location, the other nine hundred and ninety-nine must be unreal, and the choice between them is arbitrary." Student: "Then perhaps it is not located at the conditions but in the middle — in the space between the eye and what the eye sees." Teacher: "Between what and what? If it is between the eye and the colour, then it has a boundary on both sides, which makes it a substantial thing with two faces — a face toward the eye and a face toward the colour. But if it is the same on both faces, it is not in the middle but is the whole; if the two faces are different, then which face is the mind? Neither answer survives."

The student, exhausted, says: "Then the mind is non-attachment itself. It has no location because it does not attach to anything." Teacher: "If non-attachment is the mind, then non-attachment is something — it has the characteristic of non-attachment. But to have a characteristic is to be locatable as the bearer of that characteristic. Non-attachment cannot be the mind, for the same reason none of the other six locations could be."

The seven candidate locations are exhausted. None survives. The mind, considered as the discriminating consciousness that thinks itself the seat of present-moment temporal experience, has no findable place.

### II.2 The Structure of the Seven-Location Argument

The *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*, transmitted to Chang'an by the South Indian monk Pāramiti (般剌密帝) in 705 CE during the Tang dynasty and rendered into Chinese under the title *Dà fódǐng rúlái mìyīn xiūzhèng liǎoyì zhū púsà wànxíng shǒulèngyán jīng* (大佛頂如來密因修證了義諸菩薩萬行首楞嚴經, Taishō 945), opens its first fascicle with an extended dialogue between the Buddha and Ānanda — Ānanda the cousin and personal attendant of the Buddha, the foremost in the assembly of Buddhist memorisation, the figure whose retentive memory (smṛti) is the canonical literary device by which the recorded discourses of the Buddha are presented as having survived the Buddha's parinirvāṇa.

Ānanda begins by failing. He has been entrapped by the daughter of the matriarch Mātaṅgī through a heretical mantra; the Buddha sends Mañjuśrī to rescue him. When Ānanda is brought back to the Jeta Grove, he confesses to the Buddha that his great learning has not protected him against this kind of seduction, and he asks for instruction in the path of *samādhi* (absorption, 三昧) that will produce actual transformation rather than mere intellectual mastery. The Buddha agrees and begins by interrogating Ānanda concerning the location of the mind. The interrogation is not a debating exercise. It is the foundational diagnostic that the entire rest of the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* — its account of the True Mind (*zhēnxīn*, 真心) as Tathāgatagarbha, its description of the Śūraṅgama-samādhi, its enumeration of the fifty-seven bodhisattva stages, its catalogue of the fifty demonic states (*wǔyīn móshì*, 五陰魔事) that arise during meditation — depends upon. The diagnostic must produce the recognition that Ānanda's own discriminating consciousness, the consciousness with which he has been so brilliantly memorising the Buddha's teaching for years, is not the True Mind but rather the false discriminating consciousness (*wàngxīn*, 妄心), and that until Ānanda recognises this he cannot make any genuine progress in the path.

The seven candidate locations are presented in a precise sequence. Each is the most natural next answer that occurs to Ānanda when the previous one has been refuted; each refutation establishes a structural feature that the subsequent candidate must avoid; and the refutations cumulatively exhaust the logical space:

The first candidate, "the mind is inside the body," is refuted by the observation that an inside-the-body mind would see the body's interior first.

The second, "the mind is outside the body," is refuted by the unity of bodily sensation with mental experience: an outside-the-body mind would not feel the body's pinches.

The third, "the mind is hidden in the eye-faculty as in a transparent crystal cup," is refuted by the observation that the cup itself would be visible to the eye.

The fourth, "the mind is in the darkness of closed eyes," is refuted by the symmetric observation that on opening the eyes the mind would see the body's interior.

The fifth, "the mind is wherever it conjoins with conditions," is refuted by the multiplication-of-locations problem: a mind at every condition is split into many minds.

The sixth, "the mind is in the middle between," is refuted by the requirement that "the middle" must specify two endpoints, and the analysis of the two-faced middle yields a contradiction.

The seventh, "non-attachment itself is the mind," is refuted by the observation that non-attachment must have the characteristic of non-attachment, and a thing with characteristics is locatable.

The seven cover the logical space of where a substantial discriminating consciousness could be located. None survives. The diagnostic conclusion: the discriminating consciousness has no findable location. The search for a place where the mind is is not merely empirically unsuccessful; it is structurally incoherent. The mind, on the *Śūraṅgama* analysis, is not the kind of thing that has a location.

### II.3 The Implication for Time

The *Śūraṅgama* result has direct structural implications for the temporal architecture of consciousness. If the discriminating consciousness has no findable location, then the lived "now" — the present moment that the discriminating consciousness presents to itself as its own central temporal locus — cannot be pinned to any particular region of space, body, brain, or substrate. The conventional account, on which the present moment is "where" experience is happening "now," collapses: there is no where at which the experience is happening; the experience does not have a place to be.

This is not the negation of present-moment experience. The *Śūraṅgama* is unambiguous that present-moment experience occurs; what it denies is that the experience can be substantively located. The True Mind, the Tathāgatagarbha (如來藏, "matrix of the Thus-Come-One"), is not a located thing either — it is a different kind of thing, prior to the located/non-located distinction — but the False Mind, the discriminating consciousness that thinks itself the seat of experience, is unlocatable in the sense that all of its candidate locations fail.

The contemporary recovery of this result is the Cogitate Consortium's 2025 *Nature* paper, *Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness*. The paper, the largest pre-registered adversarial collaboration in the history of consciousness research (Cogitate Consortium, 256 human participants, multimodal recording across fMRI, MEG, and intracranial EEG, with prediction-and-interpretation pre-committed by the proponents of the two competing theories), tested the predictions of Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (Dehaene-Changeux, prefrontal-cortical ignition as the locus of conscious access) and Integrated Information Theory (Tononi, posterior cortical integration as the substrate of consciousness). The result, published in *Nature* 642, 8066, 133–142, 2025: neither theory's strongest predictions were confirmed. GNWT was challenged by the general lack of ignition at stimulus offset and by the limited representation of certain conscious dimensions in the prefrontal cortex; IIT was challenged by the lack of sustained synchronisation within the posterior cortex. The conclusion is not that consciousness has no neural substrate; the conclusion is that no single brain region — neither the prefrontal cortex of GNWT nor the posterior cortical region of IIT — is alone sufficient for the conscious-temporal experience that integrates over the persistence of a stimulus.

The Cogitate finding is the *Śūraṅgama* result reproduced thirteen centuries later by adversarial-collaboration cognitive neuroscience. The mind is not in the prefrontal cortex; the mind is not in the posterior cortex; the mind is not exclusively in the integrated information of any one region. None of the candidate brain-locations exhaustively grounds the conscious-temporal experience. The lived now does not have a place at which it is.

## Part III · The Non-Dual Silence (Vimalakīrti)

### III.1 Thought Experiment: The Room of Bodhisattvas

Imagine a room. The room belongs to Vimalakīrti (維摩詰), a wealthy lay-practitioner of Vaiśālī, the great commercial centre of the middle Ganges plain in the period roughly contemporary with the canonical Pali sūtras of early Buddhism. Vimalakīrti is reputed throughout the city for his combination of worldly success — he has a household, family, fortune, social standing — with extraordinary spiritual attainment. He is said to enter brothels to teach detachment, gambling halls to teach ethics, government offices to teach justice. He is, to use the technical Mahāyāna term, a layperson-bodhisattva (在家菩薩), and the *Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa Sūtra* is the founding canonical text on the layperson-bodhisattva path.

Vimalakīrti has feigned an illness. The Buddha, still in residence at the mango grove of Āmrapālī outside Vaiśālī, asks each of his most accomplished disciples in turn to visit Vimalakīrti and inquire after his health. Each disciple — Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Kāśyapa, Subhūti, Pūrṇa, Kātyāyana, Aniruddha, Upāli, Rāhula, Ānanda, and the remaining members of the inner circle of the *śrāvaka* (hearer) order — refuses, each on the grounds of an embarrassing past encounter in which Vimalakīrti has corrected their understanding of some doctrinal point. Then the Buddha asks the bodhisattvas, the Mahāyāna practitioners of the bodhisattva-path. They too refuse, one after another, each citing a past defeat at Vimalakīrti's hands. Finally Mañjuśrī (文殊師利), the bodhisattva of wisdom, accepts. He goes to Vimalakīrti's house. The entire assembly, monks and bodhisattvas and gods and layfolk, follows.

Vimalakīrti's room is small. Yet, as the entire assembly enters, the room expands miraculously to hold all of them, with countless lion-thrones imported from a distant Buddha-field on which each visitor sits comfortably. The dialogue between Vimalakīrti and Mañjuśrī unfolds across the central chapters of the sūtra, covering inconceivable liberation, the bodhisattva's illness, the goddess who confounds Śāriputra by reversing his gender, the dharma-feast brought from a distant Buddha-realm. Then, in Chapter 8 (or, in Kumārajīva's chaptering, Chapter 9), the topic turns to the entry into the dharma-door of non-duality. The bodhisattvas in attendance, one after another, give their accounts:

"Production and extinction are two; if dharmas were originally unproduced, they cannot be extinguished. Realising this is the entry into the dharma-door of non-duality." — Bodhisattva Dharmadhvaja.

"I and what is mine are two; the ego-grasping that posits an 'I' is the source of the 'mine.' If there is no presumption of a self, there will be no trace of covetousness. This absence of presumption is the door to non-duality." — Bodhisattva Śrīgandha.

"Purity and defilement are two. It is a conceited self that runs from one and clings to the other. When all conceit is laid down, then one enters the door of non-duality." — Bodhisattva Śrīkūṭa.

The accounts continue. Bodhisattva after bodhisattva, each more refined than the previous, each pointing to a particular pair of opposites and explaining how the recognition of their mutual constitution dissolves the apparent duality. By the time Mañjuśrī's turn arrives, thirty-one bodhisattvas have spoken. Mañjuśrī gives the most refined answer of all:

"In all things, no words, no speech, no demonstration, no recognition; to leave behind all questions and answers — this is to enter the dharma-door of non-duality."

Mañjuśrī's answer is the highest verbal answer. It is the answer that points beyond words by means of words; it is the negation of the very speech-act that delivers it. It is the answer of the Heart Sūtra ("no eye, no ear, no nose, no tongue, no body, no mind"), of the Diamond Sūtra ("a bodhisattva who has the notion of a self is not a bodhisattva"), of the entire *prajñāpāramitā* literature in which negation is taken to its limit. Then Mañjuśrī turns to Vimalakīrti and says: "Now, sir, what is your entry into the dharma-door of non-duality?"

Vimalakīrti is silent.

The silence is the climactic moment of the sūtra. It is depicted in the murals of Cave 220 at Dunhuang (Tang dynasty, c. 642 CE) and in countless subsequent paintings across China, Korea, and Japan, including the great Tang-and-Song paintings now held at the Tokyo National Museum and the Cleveland Museum of Art. It is the founding gesture of the Chan and Zen traditions. Mañjuśrī says, after the silence: "Excellent, excellent. Not even a syllable, not so much as a word — this is the true entry into the dharma-door of non-duality."

### III.2 The Structure of the Silence

The silence is not a refusal to speak. It is not Vimalakīrti's failure to come up with an answer; it is not his polite deferral to Mañjuśrī; it is not his exhaustion after a long day of teaching. It is, on the precise reading that the sūtra makes explicit through Mañjuśrī's commendation, the only answer that does not, by the act of being given, fall back into the duality it is trying to dissolve.

The structure of the failure of every verbal answer is precise. To enter non-duality through speech, one must say something. To say something is to produce an utterance, the content of which differs from the speaker who utters it; this is the first duality, between the speaker and the spoken-of. The content of the utterance, in turn, must refer to something other than the utterance itself; this is the second duality, between the words and what the words point at. The very act of giving a verbal answer, however refined, instantiates these two dualities. Even Mañjuśrī's "no words, no speech, no demonstration, no recognition" uses words to deny words, demonstrates by means of demonstration that no demonstration is required, recognises that no recognition is needed. The negation cannot exit the dualistic structure that the act of speech presupposes.

Vimalakīrti's silence does. The silence does not divide a speaker from a spoken-of; there is no spoken-of, because nothing is spoken. The silence does not divide words from what the words point at; there are no words, and nothing being pointed at. The silence is the only verbal-or-non-verbal answer that operationally instantiates the non-dual structure rather than verbally describing it. It is not the description of non-duality; it is non-duality enacted.

### III.3 The Silence and the Foundational Substrate of Time

The implication for time is foundational. Every account of time — every philosophical theory, every physical model, every phenomenological description — proceeds by speech. It says something about time. By the act of saying, it instantiates the speaker-spoken-of duality at the level of the utterance. The temporal substrate that the account purports to describe is, in the act of being described, separated from the act of describing it. The account is, at its own foundational level, dualistic; and any dualistic account of a non-dual substrate is, in the *Vimalakīrti* sense, partial.

This is not the rejection of all temporal accounts. The *Vimalakīrti* tradition does not say that there should be no philosophy of time, no physics of time, no phenomenology of time. The thirty-one bodhisattvas who give verbal accounts of non-duality are not corrected by Vimalakīrti; their accounts are commended by him. What the silence adds is the recognition that no verbal account is the entry into non-duality, that all verbal accounts are partial in a structural way, and that the foundational gesture of any practice that wishes to actually enter the non-dual substrate must include the moment of silence in which the verbal apparatus is set down.

The contemporary recovery of this result is the Wheeler–DeWitt equation of quantum gravity, the foundational equation of canonical quantum cosmology, written by Bryce DeWitt in 1967 and developed across the subsequent half-century by Wheeler, Hartle, Hawking, Page, Wootters, Rovelli, Höhn, Chataignier, and the broader quantum-cosmology community. The Wheeler–DeWitt equation, in its starkest form, says:

ĤΨ[g] = 0

The universal wavefunction Ψ, considered as a functional of the spatial three-metric g, is annihilated by the global Hamiltonian operator Ĥ. The equation contains no parameter "time"; the wavefunction does not evolve; the universe is, at the foundational quantum-cosmological level, timeless. This is not Wheeler's eccentricity; it is the strict consequence of imposing the canonical quantum-mechanical formalism on the constrained Hamiltonian structure of general relativity. The temporal predication that constitutes ordinary physical experience — the saying that "the universe is now in such-and-such a state, will be in such-and-such a state later, was in such-and-such a state earlier" — is not a feature of the foundational equation. It is a feature that emerges only when an observer-subsystem is identified within the total quantum state and the time of that observer-subsystem is reconstructed from its correlations with the rest of the universe via the Page–Wootters mechanism (D. N. Page and W. K. Wootters, *Evolution without evolution: Dynamics described by stationary observables*, Physical Review D 27, 2885, 1983; W. K. Wootters, *"Time" replaced by quantum correlations*, International Journal of Theoretical Physics 23, 701, 1984).

The Page–Wootters mechanism, developed in 1983 and subjected to vigorous refinement and experimental simulation across the subsequent forty years (with major recent contributions in L. Chataignier, P. A. Höhn, M. P. E. Lock, F. M. Mele, *Relational dynamics with periodic clocks*, *New Journal of Physics* 28, 034504, 2026; the 2025 *Observers and Timekeepers: From the Page-Wootters Mechanism to the Gravitational Path Integral*, arXiv:2506.21489; and the December 2025 *Relational Emergent Time for Quantum System: A Multi-Observer, Gravitational, and Cosmological Framework*, arXiv:2512.15789), is the foundational-physics analogue of the Vimalakīrti silence. Time is recovered only as the relational structure of correlations between an observer-clock subsystem and the rest of the universe; outside that relational structure, at the level of the total state, no temporal account is available. The 2026 *New Scientist* treatment of this result reflects the broader recognition, across foundations of physics, that time as a foundational entity has been steadily eroded across the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries — Mach's relational mechanics, Einstein's relativistic spacetime, Wheeler-DeWitt's timeless wavefunction, Page-Wootters's correlation-emergent time, Rovelli's relational interpretation, the recent Chataignier-Höhn-Lock-Mele extension to periodic clocks. The progression is a steady stripping-away of substantive time, ending in the recognition that the foundational substrate is silent and that all temporal accounts are observer-relational.

The Vimalakīrti silence is the *prajñā-pāramitā* enactment of this physics result, two thousand years before the physics. The temporal substrate is, at its foundation, prior to the speaker-spoken-of duality and prior to the temporal predication that presupposes that duality. Every account of time is partial. The full entry requires the moment of silence in which the verbal apparatus is set down.

## Part IV · The 2025–2026 Convergence in the Cognitive Science of Time

### IV.1 The Consciousness-of-Time Substrate: Cogitate, Active Inference, and Temporal Thickness

The contemporary cognitive science of conscious temporal experience converged, in 2025–2026, on three results that jointly recover the *Śūraṅgama* no-place finding at the empirical scale.

The pre-registered adversarial collaboration between proponents of Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (the Dehaene-Changeux camp) and Integrated Information Theory (the Tononi-Koch camp) tested the divergent predictions of the two theories using a theory-neutral consortium of experimental laboratories, with prediction-and-interpretation committed in advance. The finding: neither theory's strong predictions were confirmed. The conscious-temporal experience that integrates over the persistence of a stimulus does not exclusively occupy any single brain region; it is not pinned to the prefrontal cortex; it is not pinned to the posterior cortex; it does not have a single locus.

The second result is the active-inference and predictive-processing program developed by Karl Friston, Jakob Hohwy, Andy Clark, Anil Seth, Ryan Smith, Adam Whyte, and the broader 2020–2026 community working on the Free Energy Principle and its applications to consciousness. The central finding: subjective awareness is grounded in the *temporal thickness* — also called *counterfactual depth* — of the agent's generative model. Conscious experience is not a feature of any particular instantaneous brain state; it is a feature of the depth and breadth of the temporally extended generative model that the brain is using to predict the world's evolution and to select actions. An agent with a thick, counterfactually-deep generative model — one that integrates over substantial past evidence and projects substantial counterfactual possible futures — has conscious experience; an agent with a thin generative model does not. The 2026 J. Hohwy, A. Corcoran, et al. *Integrated Information and Predictive Processing Theories of Consciousness: An Adversarial Collaborative Review* extends and integrates this with the IIT-vs-GNWT empirical findings.

The third result is the converging recognition, across phenomenology, philosophy of mind, and contemporary cognitive science, that the lived "now" is not the punctual zero-duration instant of classical metaphysics but the *temporally thick* structure that the Husserlian retention–primal-impression–protention triad already identified (Husserl, *Vorlesungen zur Phänomenologie des inneren Zeitbewusstseins*, posthumously edited from 1905–1928 lectures by Heidegger, 1928) and that the Varela-Thompson neurophenomenology program (F. The lived now, on this lineage's account, is a temporally thick window of approximately 100 milliseconds to 3 seconds, within which retention of just-passed experience and protention of about-to-come experience are simultaneously co-present with the primal impression, and within which the conscious temporal integration that makes possible perception, action, and self-recognition occurs.

The three results converge on the *Śūraṅgama*-style conclusion. The lived now is not a punctual instant pinned to a particular brain region. It is a temporally thick, observer-relational structure that integrates retention, primal impression, and protention across a window whose neural realisation is distributed across multiple cortical and subcortical regions and whose computational realisation requires the counterfactual-depth integration of the agent's generative model with its surrounding world. The mind has no place; the temporal experience has no place; the substrate is, at the finest empirical resolution that 2025-2026 cognitive science has so far delivered, irreducibly distributed and irreducibly relational.

## Closing Meditation

Three sūtras, three discoveries about the architecture of consciousness-and-time, three operational specifications that, when properly integrated, exhaust the dimensional space of what consciousness-only time can be.

The *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*, composed in stages between approximately the third and fifth centuries of the common era and transmitted to East Asia through three successive Chinese translations across the next three centuries, articulated the storehouse-consciousness substrate. The image is the ocean and the wind: an ocean of mind, perfectly still in itself, on the surface of which the seven active consciousnesses arise as waves stirred by the wind of action. The waves are real; they have measurable structure; they break on shores and erode them. But they are not the ocean. The ocean — the ālaya-vijñāna, the storehouse-consciousness, the eighth — contains the seeds (bīja) and habit-energies (vāsanā) of all past experience and grounds the conditions under which the waves continue to arise. The architecture is foundationally temporal: the seeds are deposits from past experience; their ripening produces present experience; the continuity that holds the practitioner together across moments is the persistence of the storehouse beneath the changing waves; the transformation that the contemplative practice can effect is a reorganisation of the storehouse itself. Time, on the *Laṅkāvatāra* analysis, is the differential structure of the storehouse — the difference between what has been deposited and what has not, between what is ripening and what remains dormant.

The *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*, transmitted to Chang'an by the South Indian monk Pāramiti in 705 CE during the Tang dynasty, opens with the Buddha's interrogation of Ānanda concerning the location of the mind. Seven candidate locations are proposed, one after another, by Ānanda, the cousin and personal attendant of the Buddha and the most learned of the early Buddhist memorisers. Each location, on examination, fails. The mind is not in the body; not outside the body; not hidden in the eye-faculty as in a transparent crystal cup; not in the darkness of closed eyes; not wherever it conjoins with conditions; not in the middle between any two things; not in non-attachment itself. The seven exhaust the logical space of where a substantial discriminating consciousness could be located. None survives. The diagnostic conclusion is structural: the discriminating consciousness has no findable place. The implication for time is direct: the lived "now," considered as the central temporal locus of the discriminating consciousness, cannot be substantively pinned to any region of space, body, brain, or substrate.

The *Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa Sūtra*, composed in Sanskrit around the first century of the common era and translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva in 406 CE, presents the assembled bodhisattvas in Vimalakīrti's small but miraculously expanded room, each in turn giving an account of the entry into the dharma-door of non-duality. The accounts are increasingly refined, culminating in Mañjuśrī's statement: "in all things, no words, no speech, no demonstration, no recognition; to leave behind all questions and answers — this is to enter the dharma-door of non-duality." It is the highest possible verbal account, the verbal approach that takes negation to its limit. Then Mañjuśrī turns to Vimalakīrti and asks for his answer. Vimalakīrti is silent. The thunderous silence, depicted in the Dunhuang murals and in countless paintings since, is not refusal. It is the only answer that does not, by the act of being given, fall back into the duality between speaker and spoken-of, between words and what the words point at. The silence is the operational entry into non-duality, not the verbal description of it. The implication for the foundational substrate of time is direct: every verbal account of time, however refined, is partial; the foundational substrate is silent, prior to the temporal predication that presupposes the speaker-spoken-of duality.
